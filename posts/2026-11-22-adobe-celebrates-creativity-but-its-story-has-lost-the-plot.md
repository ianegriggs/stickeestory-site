## While the company champions what creators can achieve, it's overlooking what they have to endure.

On a typical agency afternoon, an art director has finished a campaign design. The client is waiting for approval, but the correct brand asset is buried three revisions deep in a feedback thread. The creative work is complete. But the administrative work keeps mounting.

For decades, Adobe has owned the promise of possibility. However, the daily obstacles facing the people who use its tools still struggle to find a place in its larger story. Now, with analysts questioning the strength of its competitive moat, perhaps the strongest narrative Adobe could tell begins where its current one stops?

### What Adobe's story leaves out

The conventional explanation for Adobe's current challenge is growing competition. [Wall Street analysts have become increasingly bearish](https://archive.is/20261016163703/https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-16/adobe-analysts-have-never-been-so-bearish-as-ai-fears-mount), citing threats from AI and cheaper web-based tools. Those concerns are valid, particularly as creative workflows become less dependent on individual software suites. But there's another issue worth examining, and it's one Adobe has far more control over: the story it tells.

[Adobe's message is pretty straightforward](https://www.adobe.com/about-adobe.html): everyone should have the power to imagine, create, and bring their ideas to life. And Adobe has plenty to show for it. Since Firefly launched in March 2023, [more than 24 billion assets have been generated](https://tech.yahoo.com/ai/articles/adobe-firefly-ai-generated-24-084000474.html).

The problem is, its protagonist, the person whose experience gives the narrative meaning, remains undefined. Everyone is the audience, but no one is a character. Without someone specific to follow, creative freedom remains a corporate promise rather than a story people can see themselves in.

To Adobe's credit, the company is beginning to address the problems behind that missing narrative. At its November 2026 MAX conference, it [announced AI agents and new integrations designed to connect tools, automate repetitive tasks, and give creatives more time for their craft](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/11/adobe-transforms-creative-cloud-tools). Its [latest product messaging](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2026/11/10/explore-new-ways-create-across-creative-cloud-adobe-firefly-while-advancing-craft-precision-choice-control) even acknowledges how much time gets lost to the work surrounding the work.

But addressing these problems in product announcements is only part of the story. As [Adobe expands its tools across AI models and third-party platforms](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/10/adobe-adds-more-ai-to-its-products-and-launches-an-mcp/), its overarching promise still emphasizes what people can create. The daily struggle to get there deserves a much bigger role, and making that struggle central to the message could give people a stronger reason to care.

So maybe it's time Adobe started telling a story about what's getting in people's way.

### How I'd reframe it

**The Job Is Finished. So Why Are You Still At It?**

It's 11 PM, and the final campaign export has crashed. Or the final approval is still stuck somewhere between the client and the production team. Either way, an exhausted creative director is troubleshooting a project that was finished hours ago. And this isn't bad luck. It's the hidden tax on creative work being collected long after the job is done.

The worst part is how much of this goes unnoticed. Every unnecessary handoff, every round of chasing approvals, every hour spent fixing something that should already work takes time away from what designers are paid for and passionate about: their ideas.

And that should make creatives angry. Years spent developing an eye for composition, typography, and storytelling, only to waste their evenings dealing with administrative tasks and logistical issues. Call it systemic theft. The tools meant to make creatives' lives easier are stealing time from the very work they're supposed to support.

Here's a story Adobe could tell instead, building on the direction it's already taking: creatives get to spend their time bringing their ideas to life, not trying to keep disconnected tools and processes working together.

Imagine finishing a project and knowing the hard part is actually over. The brief becomes an asset. The asset gets approved. The approved work goes live, ready to measure, without another round of chasing files or fixing problems.

The work moves forward and the people behind it move on.

For creatives, the payoff is worth more than another software feature. It's the freedom to focus on the kind of thinking that actually requires a human mind.

> No designer should have to work overtime simply because their AI tools don't talk to each other.

### What makes it Stickee

The proposed story draws on a psychological principle known as the Peak-End Rule, described by psychologists [Barbara L. Fredrickson](https://www.linkedin.com/in/barbara-l-fredrickson-4426425a/) and Daniel Kahneman. Their research shows how especially intense moments and the ending of an experience can disproportionately shape how people remember and evaluate it. 

The 11 PM export failure gives us the painful peak. The prospect of finishing a project and actually being able to move on provides a very different ending. Together, they give Adobe a narrative built around a frustration its audience can recognize and a resolution worth caring about.

That's the opportunity for Adobe as I see it. Give its audience someone to recognize, a frustration they know all too well, and a reason to believe there's a better way. Build the story around what creative professionals want to get back, and it stands a better chance of resonating.
